## A Testable Prediction Framework for AST

Affective Survival Theory becomes much more scientifically tractable when it is framed as a comparative, falsifiable computational model rather than a direct neural readout of consciousness. That is also the direction the field is moving: recent large-scale adversarial collaborations in consciousness science explicitly pit theories against one another using preregistered multimodal predictions, and recent methodological discussions argue that theory progress depends on clear operationalisation and head-to-head testing.

AST’s distinctive claim is that conscious salience should depend not only on predictive credibility or surprise, but also on the joint effects of affective intensity, survival relevance, valence, and perceived probability, plus memory and anticipation. That makes the AST experimentally useful because it yields predictions that can differ from simpler prediction-error or posterior-only accounts. At the same time, current EEG-based affect decoding and multimodal fusion methods make it increasingly feasible to estimate latent variables of this sort from combined neural, behavioural, and physiological data.

Formal model

AST conceptual equation:<br/>
C_t=α∑_(k=1)^n( A_k×S_k×V_k×P_k)+βΨ_histoty+γΦ_future<br/>

where:<br/>
A_k= affective intensity<br/>
S_k= survival relevance<br/>
V_k= affective valence<br/>
P_k= perceived/predicted probability<br/> 
Ψ_history= memory contribution<br/>
Φ_future= anticipation contribution<br/>

For empirical work, these are treated as operationalised latent variables, not raw scanner outputs. EEG can contribute fast trial-level signal features and affect-sensitive decoding; multimodal fusion can combine complementary signals; and task structure plus behaviour can define survival relevance, memory, and anticipation terms.

## Conceptual computational phenotype - not a real clinical diagnostic instrument.
## A research framework for modelling psychosis-related distortions in the AST-Bayesian system


> [!CAUTION]
> Important caution

I would strongly avoid calling this an actual diagnostic of psychosis in a clinical sense right now.

Psychosis diagnosis remains clinical, and current computational models are not validated replacements for psychiatric assessment.

We can build an “AST diagnostic signature” of psychosis as a pattern of mathematical distortions in the AST-Bayesian model, but today it would be a research framework, not a validated diagnostic test. 

Current clinical diagnosis of psychosis is still based on symptoms, history, risk assessment, and clinical evaluation, not on a single computational equation. NICE guidance for psychosis and schizophrenia is built around recognition, assessment, and management rather than biomarker-based diagnosis.

That said, computational psychiatry does already model hallucinations and delusions in Bayesian / predictive-processing terms, including altered priors, altered sensory likelihoods, and abnormal precision weighting. Reviews also note that hallucinations and delusions may arise from distinct inferential abnormalities rather than a single mechanism.

AST does not diagnose psychosis directly. Instead, it proposes that psychosis-like phenomena may appear as systematic parameter errors inside a survival-weighted Bayesian consciousness model.

1. Starting from the AST-Bayesian equation

C_t=α∑_(k=1)^n( A_k×S_k×V_k×P_k)+βΨ_past+γΦ_future

where: <br/>
A_k= affective intensity <br/>
S_k= survival relevance <br/>
V_k= affective valence <br/>
P_k= perceived/predicted probability  <br/>
Ψ_past= past / memory contribution <br/>
Φ_future= future / anticipatory contribution<br/> 
and:<br/>
P_k=P(H_k∣D_t)

where H_kis the hypothesis that signal k reflects a real survival-relevant state.

use α=β=γ=1 for simplicity in this example

2. What “psychosis as mathematical error” would mean

In a normal case, the parameters stay roughly calibrated to reality.
In a psychosis-like case, the model becomes miscalibrated.
That miscalibration could appear as one or more of the following conceptual errors:

## Error 1: inflated perceived/predicted probability probability P_k

The model assigns a very high posterior to a threat, voice, or meaning hypothesis despite weak or ambiguous evidence.
This matches the general computational idea that delusions and hallucinations involve abnormal inference, and delusions are classically defined as beliefs held with high certainty despite contradictory evidence.

## Error 2: exaggerated affective intensity A_k
A weak or ambiguous cue is felt with excessive emotional force.

## Error 3: exaggerated survival relevance S_k
A neutral or low-stakes event is treated as if it has extreme personal importance, danger, or existential weight.

## Error 4: distorted valence V_k
Neutral cues become strongly negative, threatening, accusatory, grandiose, or unusually meaningful.

## Error 5: over-weighted past term Ψ_past
Trauma, prior fear, or strongly learned associations dominate present interpretation.

## Error 6: over-weighted future term Φ_future
Anticipation becomes catastrophically amplified: “this means something terrible is about to happen.”

So, the AST picture of psychosis would not be “the person is irrational” in a vague sense. It would be:
the inferential-affective system is overweighting certain hypotheses and/or overweighting their emotional and survival significance.

## 3. A simple psychosis-signature proposal

We could define a conceptual AST Psychosis Distortion Index for research: <br/>
Δ_AST=w_1 ΔP_k+w_2 ΔA_k+w_3 ΔS_k+w_4 ΔV_k+w_5 ΔΨ+w_6 ΔΦ <br/>

where each Δterm is the deviation from a normative or control-calibrated value. <br/>
For example: <br/>
ΔP_k: posterior too high for weak evidence <br/>
ΔA_k: emotional intensity too high for the cue <br/>
ΔS_k: survival/personal relevance too high <br/>
ΔV_k: negative or grandiose value distortion <br/>
ΔΨ: excessive past-driven weighting <br/>
ΔΦ: excessive anticipatory weighting <br/>
This would be a research score, not a diagnosis.

## 4. A falsifiable research prediction

A strong AST prediction would be:<br/>
People with psychosis-spectrum symptoms will show larger mismatches between evidence quality and posterior / salience assignment than controls, especially for ambiguous but affectively charged stimuli.

That is testable.

More specifically: <br/>
with matched ambiguity, <br/>
psychosis-spectrum participants should show higher P_k, <br/>
stronger A_k, <br/>
stronger S_k, <br/>
and therefore, higher predicted conscious salience C_t, <br/>
especially for threat-like or self-referential stimuli. <br/> 
That sits naturally beside existing predictive-processing accounts of psychosis. <br/>

## 5. Step-by-step worked example

I’ll show a control-like case and a psychosis-like case using the same ambiguous evidence. <br/>
### Scenario

The participant hears a vague, noise-like auditory stimulus in a quiet room.  <br/>
Hypothesis: <br/>
H_k="“this sound is a real threatening voice directed at me”" 

That sits naturally beside existing predictive-processing accounts of psychosis. <br/>
Evidence:  <br/>
D_t="“brief ambiguous sound fragment”" 

We calculate: <br/>
P_k=P(H_k∣D_t)

using Bayes’ theorem.

## 6. Control-like calculation

Assume: <br/>
prior probability of a threatening voice: <br/>
P(H_k)=0.10

likelihood of this evidence if it really is a threatening voice:<br/> 
P(D_t∣H_k)=0.60

likelihood of this evidence if it is not a threatening voice:<br/> 
P(D_t∣¬H_k)=0.40

Then: <br/>
P(H_k∣D_t)=(P(D_t∣H_k)P(H_k))/(P(D_t∣H_k)P(H_k)+P(D_t∣¬H_k)P(¬H_k))

Substitute:<br/>
P(H_k∣D_t)=((0.60)(0.10))/((0.60)(0.10)+(0.40)(0.90) )

0.06/(0.06+0.36)

0.06/0.42


So:<br/>
(P_k^control=0.1429)

Now choose AST terms:<br/>
A_k=0.30S_k=0.20V_k=0.30Ψ_past=0.10Φ_future=0.10<br/>
Then:<br/>
C^control=(0.30×0.20×0.30×0.1429)+0.10+0.10

Step by step:

0.30×0.20=0.060.06×0.30=0.0180.018×0.1429=0.0025722C^control=0.0025722+0.10+0.10

(C^control=0.2026)

So the ambiguous sound remains low in conscious salience.

## 7. Psychosis-like calculation

Now keep the same evidence, but assume the system is miscalibrated. <br/>
Conceptually, this could mean:<br/>
stronger prior for threat/self-reference,<br/> 
excessive weighting of affect, <br/>
excessive survival relevance, <br/>
inflated anticipation. 

Let:<br/>
prior probability: <br/>
P(H_k)=0.45

same likelihood if true: 
P(D_t∣H_k)=0.60

but reduced tolerance for non-threatening explanation: 
P(D_t∣¬H_k)=0.20

Then

P(H_k∣D_t)=((0.60)(0.45))/((0.60)(0.45)+(0.20)(0.55) )
					
0.27/(0.27+0.11)

0.27/0.38

So:
(P_k^(psychosis-like)=0.7105)

Now use amplified AST terms:

A_k=0.85S_k=0.90V_k=0.90Ψ_past=0.40Φ_future=0.70

Then:

C^(psychosis-like)=(0.85×0.90×0.90×0.7105)+0.40+0.70

C^(psychosis-like)=0.4898+0.40+0.70

C^(psychosis-like)=1.5898

## 8. Interpretation

Using the same ambiguous sound: <br/>
control-like model: <br/>
C≈0.20

psychosis-like model: <br/>
C≈1.59

Difference:<br/>
1.5898-0.2026=1.3872

So the model predicts that the same weak evidence could become a highly salient, threatening conscious experience if: <br/>
posterior probability is inflated, <br/>
affective intensity is exaggerated, <br/>
survival relevance is over-assigned, <br/>
past and future weighting are amplified. 

That is the mathematical sense in which psychosis could appear in AST as a miscalibrated survival-consciousness system.

## 9. How to test this empirically

A falsifiable experiment could use:<br/>
ambiguous auditory or visual stimuli,<br/> 
trial-by-trial ratings of certainty, threat, and salience, <br/>
EEG for fast salience processing, <br/>
fMRI / decoding for threat- or self-referential network engagement, <br/>
computational fitting of P_k,A_k,S_k,V_k,Ψ,Φ. 

Prediction: <br/>
psychosis-spectrum participants should show larger fitted distortions in posterior probability and affective-survival <br/>
weighting than controls, especially under ambiguity.<br/>
If those distortions do not appear, the AST psychosis framework is weakened.

Within an AST-Bayesian framework, psychosis may be modelled not as a simple excess of error, but as a distortion of affective-survival inference. Ambiguous cues may acquire abnormally high posterior credibility, exaggerated affective intensity, inflated survival relevance, and excessive weighting by memory and anticipation, thereby generating highly salient conscious states out of weak or equivocal evidence. Such a framework would not constitute a clinical diagnostic tool in its current form, but it could serve as a computational research model for psychosis-related miscalibration in conscious inference.
